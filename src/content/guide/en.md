Interview Compass is a bilingual question library for engineering interviews, promotion, and project review. Use it to compare approaches, trace personal contribution, and explain results with evidence. Open-ended questions and evidence references support a focused discussion.

The library covers software, frontend, backend, mobile, AI, and data engineering across individual-contributor and engineering-management roles. Product, operations, design, and commercial topics add business context to engineering work. Assess each person’s contribution within their responsibilities and authority.

## Role and stage targets

Choose a role and level of responsibility to prepare for or assess, or browse all questions. Use the control in the upper-right corner to change your selection at any time. Lists, previews, counts, and search use reviewed role-and-stage combinations; topic and format filters narrow the results further. Clear the filters or browse all questions if no results match.

One question may apply at several career stages. Compare the responsibility, judgment, and outcome evidence expected at each stage. Stage comparisons are separate from the selected target, and direct links remain available with their scope shown.

Your selection is saved in this browser. Choose **Browse all questions** to save that view, or dismiss the selector for this session. Use **Copy filtered link** to share your target and filters. Recipients can use the linked target, keep their saved default, or remove the link target.

## How questions are organized

Every question has exactly one **primary capability**: the one thing it is mainly meant to examine. The eight domains are: A professional problem solving and implementation, B business understanding and domain modeling, C value judgment and direction setting, D delivery and ongoing operations, E collaboration, communication, and influence, F people and organization building, G learning, adaptation, and sustained growth, and H professional responsibility and work style.

Roles, topics, responsibility scopes, career tracks, use cases, and formats are independent views into the same questions.

## Questioning methods

Interviewers start with the role’s goals and responsibility scope, then use contrasting experiences, option comparisons, causal probes, and practical details to examine experience and judgment. For setbacks or conflict, ask about context, actions, outcomes, and later changes. Follow-ups add context, test explanations, and clarify contribution.

Candidates can start with a real situation and explain the goal, responsibilities, constraints, actions, alternatives, and trade-offs, then show the outcome, their contribution, and what they learned. STAR can organize an example; technical, case, and system-design questions benefit from direct reasoning, comparisons, and evidence. See the [interviewing guide](../framework/) for follow-ups, answer structure, and questions to ask employers.

## External interviews and internal promotion

An external interview asks whether someone can perform the target role. A promotion review asks whether they have sustained a wider scope of responsibility over time and produced verifiable, attributable results. The two can reuse questions, but they require different preparation and evidence horizons.

The promotion page offers question paths for individual contributors, first-line managers, and leaders who manage managers. Use these paths alongside your organization’s capability model and promotion criteria for expectations, review periods, and packet formats. Prepare evidence for the target scope, sustained outcomes, how those outcomes were achieved, and supporting or contrary evidence.

## How to use a question

Questions prompt inquiry. Individually edited analysis is labeled “Question-specific analysis · reviewed”; shared reading prompts are labeled “Shared reading guide · question-specific review pending”. When a question has no dedicated analysis, start by restating the decision or uncertainty it asks you to resolve.

Useful judgments compare internal history, similar cases, industry or competitor references, alternatives, and the cost of doing nothing. State the measurement basis and its limits. Then establish the goal, baseline, constraints, responsibility, the person’s actual actions, affected parties, outcomes, and plausible alternative causes. Hands-on evidence depends on the role: look for decisions and actions within the relevant scope, not a universal requirement to code personally.

## Four evidence levels

Questions include shared 1–4 evidence references; reviewed questions may also include question-specific anchors. Use them to discuss one capability in context and examine the evidence.

- **1 · Below expectations**: Core concepts, basic facts, or the account remain unclear; for experience questions, the person cannot explain their own actions. Confirmed deliberate fabrication falls below expectations. Inconsistency or missing detail alone means the evidence needs further checking.
- **2 · Passing**: Basic concepts and reasoning are clear, with credible evidence of having done the work or a sound response to the prompt.
- **3 · Excellent**: The person gives a structured reflection, examines what worked and what did not, and shows practice or insight above the common expectation for the role and level.
- **4 · Exceptional**: The person shows a field-leading result within the relevant scope; the result is measurable, verifiable, and credibly attributable to their decisions and work.

Expectations depend on the role's capability model, level, and responsibility scope. A score of 2 is the usual baseline; excellent evidence in one or two dimensions is meaningful, while exceptional evidence should be rare.

## Recurring lenses

- **Comparison and competition:** State the comparison set, evaluation criteria, and time horizon; explain whether the differences are large enough to change a judgment or action.
- **Challenging goals and outcomes:** Explain the difficulty relative to the baseline, key constraints, outcome measures, and stop-loss conditions; describe how to adjust or stop when progress misses expectations.
- **Causality and contribution:** Clarify personal responsibility, key actions, and trade-offs; test other contributing factors and contrary evidence, distinguishing individual contribution from team results.
- **Cost and risk:** Compare implementation, maintenance, and opportunity costs; identify key assumptions, failure impact, validation methods, and fallback plans.
- **Progress and evolution:** Explain how work was staged, assumptions validated, and investment expanded over time; use evidence to decide whether to continue, adjust, or change direction.
- **Growth:** Examine learning, self-direction, thinking, and resilience through concrete behavior, not labels.
- **Assessing the role:** Candidates and employers should clarify the actual work, goals, decision-making authority, available support, and team environment before deciding whether the role is a good fit.

Resource coordination can be explored under causality and contribution or progress and evolution: focus on how the candidate coordinated dependencies, secured resources, and made trade-offs, using concrete actions and outcomes as evidence.

## Content rules

- Questions stay open-ended. The levels provide evidence references for discussion; people responsible for the role make hiring decisions.
- Every published question has Chinese and English versions with the same primary capability.
- Every question states its provenance: adapted, derived, inspired, or original to this site. No source is invented.
- Wording is distinct from source articles, and similar questions are kept apart only when they examine different evidence.
